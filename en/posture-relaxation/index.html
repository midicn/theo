"Relaxation" is the most misleading word here, because its opposite is not effort:

Relaxation is not slackness but using effort where it belongs and not where it does not.

Both extremes are wrong:

ExtremeSignsConsequence
too much tensionraised shoulders, locked wrist, clenched jaw, held breathstiff movement, quick fatigue, injury risk
too little tensioncollapsing fingers, sunken wrist, lost supportpoor control, weak sound

So the goal is not "as loose as possible" but "effort correctly distributed".

Three principles

PrincipleExplanation
1 use gravity rather than fighting itlet the arm's weight fall onto the key or string instead of pressing with muscle
2 keep joints neutralthe wrist neither cocked up nor collapsed, in a position free to move
3 keep breathingthe most overlooked, and the easiest to self-check

The third is the most practical part of this entry:

Can you breathe normally while playing? Can you speak?

Held breath is the most direct signal of tension — it often appears before you *feel* nervous, and once the breath stops, whole-body tension rises at once (the breathing muscles and the trunk are linked).

So "watch your breathing" is not mysticism but the most directly controllable relaxation measure: once the breath flows, shoulder and neck tension usually drops by itself.

Two practical self-checks

CheckHow
the shake testafter a phrase, stop and shake out the hands — if they are very stiff, there is too much tension
replay it much softerplay the same phrase at a clearly lower dynamic — if the volume barely changes, you were pressing hard all along

The second is neat: it turns "how much effort" from an inward sensation into externally observable evidence.

During practice

SituationNote
slow practicethe moment to adjust posture (see slow practice)
speeding upwatch whether tension rises linearly with speed — a steep rise means inefficient motion
when nervousdeal with the breathing first (see performance anxiety)

The second row matters: some rise of tension in hard passages is normal, but too much means the motion is inefficient — an inefficient motion demands extra muscle to compensate.

Listen: dynamics and effort

Use rhythm on a shape with obvious dynamic contrast — and notice that real dynamic change comes from distributing effort, not from trying harder. If a fortissimo differs from a pianissimo only by stiffness, that loudness loses its gradations.

Common misconceptions

  • "Relaxation means using as little effort as possible." Too little tension is equally harmful (support is

    • lost). The goal is correctly distributed effort.

  • "Tension only affects comfort." It directly affects timbre, control and accuracy — superfluous tension

    • disturbs fine motion.

  • "Watching your breathing is mysticism." Breathing muscles and the trunk are linked, so **held breath raises

    • whole-body tension at once** — the most controllable entry point.

  • "Posture is a beginner's concern." At higher levels, motion efficiency becomes decisive — it sets the

    • ceiling of technique. :::
