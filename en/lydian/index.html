Lydian Mode

Parallel major with a raised fourth, an augmented fourth above the tonic

1 min readScales and ModesUpdated 2026-11-02

theorymodechurch modes

Related

Major ScaleMixolydian ModeAugmented and Diminished IntervalsScale

Lydian has the notes of parallel major with degree 4 raised a semitone. This raised fourth often contributes a bright or suspended colour, but melody, harmony and scoring affect the result.

C major: C D E F G A B Lydian (C): C D E F♯ G A B

The raised fourth has two consequences:

1. The tonic to the fourth becomes a tritone (C–F♯, six semitones). This interval distinguishes Lydian from parallel major (see augmented and diminished). 2. The subdominant support disappears. In major, the fourth degree is the lower prop under the tonic; raise it and the prop is gone.

With C as tonic, Lydian uses F♯ and B, while Mixolydian uses F and B♭: degrees 4 and 7 both differ. The one-note comparison is with parallel major, whose fourth is raised to obtain Lydian.

Hear Lydian, then major. The only difference is the height of the fourth — one hovers, the other lands.

Common misconceptions

  • "A raised fourth is a mistake." It is the definition of Lydian. Write F instead and you have major again.
  • "Lydian is just major started elsewhere." Structurally it can be derived that way, but the centre of gravity moves: the hovering comes from the tritone between the new tonic and its fourth.
  • "A tritone must sound harsh." In Lydian it is usually handled as a soft suspension rather than a bite, depending on harmony and scoring.
  • "Lydian is rare." In film scoring and post-rock it is a standard tool for a wide, floating atmosphere.
