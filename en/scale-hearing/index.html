Hearing Key and Scale

The test is where the centre is, not which notes are used

1 min read40 characters条目Ear TrainingUpdated 2026-09-25

theoryear trainingtonality

Related

The step where key hearing most easily goes wrong is looking for "which notes are used". The right first question is another one:

Where is the centre?

A pitch set cannot determine a key: C major and A minor share the same notes (see key signature). So hearing which notes are used does not answer what key this is.

The cheapest test: the third above the tonic

TestConclusion
a major third above the tonicmajor mode
a minor third above the tonicminor mode

Why it is the cheapest: it needs only the tonic and the third, and those fix the mode's colour. Changes to the sixth and seventh are variants on that base:

VariantChange
harmonic minorthe seventh degree raised (to obtain dominant function)
melodic minorthe sixth also raised going up
Dorian, Mixolydian and othersthe sixth or seventh adjusted to the mode

So the order is: find the tonic → hear the third → then note the sixth and seventh.

How to find the centre

The centre is not a "feeling"; it is established by three things:

HowExplanation
cadencesthe strongest means of establishing a centre (see cadence)
repetition and emphasisthe note that occurs most, and longest, is often the tonic
the beginning and the endthe overwhelming majority of works end on the tonic

The most practical shortcut: listen to the last note. It is very likely the tonic (see tonal centre).

Without a cadence the centre goes vague. That is why much modern music has no audible key.

Three cases of "no audible key"

CaseCauseCan you judge?
vague centre (e.g. Impressionist)traditional cadences avoidedoften "roughly centred on some note", but not definite
frequent modulationthe centre keeps changingyes, section by section
atonalitythe centre deliberately removedno — there is no centre to find

Romantic works often pass through several keys within a minute. Ask "what key is this passage", not "what key is this". The unit of judgement follows the music's scale.

Diagram: the test is the centre, not the pitch set

Listen: one pitch set, two centres

Use scale on two scales — the same notes (C D E F G A B) but one centred on C and one on A. Notice which note carries the sense of arrival.

Common misconceptions

  • "Knowing the notes tells you the key." It does not. Relative major and minor share a pitch set; the centre must be judged.
  • "Key is a matter of feeling." There are concrete means: cadences, emphasis, and the beginning and end.
  • "Minor means sad." That is a stereotype. Minor keys have no fixed emotional meaning (see the minor scale).
  • "Not hearing a key means poor ears." Some music has no centre (atonality); failing to find one is correct.
