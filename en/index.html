Theory

A structured course in music theory and harmony — pitch, intervals, scales and modes, chords, harmonic progression, counterpoint, rhythm, melody, form, notation and performance practice. Every entry pairs playable examples with hand-drawn diagrams, all originally written in Chinese and English.

T1

  • Concert PitchA4 at 440 Hz is a recent agreement; pitch standards used to float from place to place
  • TimbreAt the same pitch, timbre comes from how strong each partial is and how the attack is shaped
  • Harmonic SeriesA single tone is not one frequency but a stack of integer multiples of one
  • RegisterRange is how far an instrument can go; register is where the music is sitting right now
  • OctaveTwo tones a frequency doubling apart are heard as the same class of note, one level higher
  • Note NameCutting continuous pitch into nameable points — letters, solfège syllables and Chinese pitch names
  • PitchFrequency sets the pitch, but the ear hears ratios, not hertz
  • What Is a Musical SoundSound is vibrating air; a musical tone is the kind whose pitch can be named and repeated

T2

  • Intervals in MelodySteps and leaps set a melody's breathing; interval width is its tone of voice
  • Interval Ear TrainingFrom anchor melodies to thinking in semitones — a training order you can keep running alone
  • EnharmonicOne key can carry several spellings — and the spelling, not the sound, carries the function
  • Consonance and DissonanceConsonance comes in degrees, and dissonance is what drives music forward — harmony rests on this ladder
  • Interval InversionRaise the lower note an octave and the degrees add to nine while the quality flips
  • Simple and Compound IntervalsIntervals within an octave are simple; anything wider than an octave is compound
  • Augmented and Diminished IntervalsWhat a perfect or major interval becomes when widened or narrowed by one more semitone
  • Perfect IntervalsUnisons, fourths, fifths and octaves are perfect rather than major or minor — their ratios are the simplest
  • Major and Minor IntervalsSame degree, one semitone apart in size — that is all that separates major from minor
  • Semitone and Whole ToneInterval size is the second ruler — it measures how many semitones lie between two notes
  • Interval NumberDegree counts letter names only, ignoring semitones — the first ruler for any interval
  • IntervalThe distance between two notes — decided by degree and by semitones

T3

  • Scales as HarmonyA scale is not just melodic material — stack thirds on it and a family of chords appears
  • Artificial ScaleNot grown from tradition but built from an idea
  • Japanese ScalesPentatonic but with semitones — insen and ryukyu build their colour from where the semitones fall
  • Octatonic ScaleWhole and half steps alternating — eight notes, two diminished sevenths stacked
  • Chromatic ScaleAll twelve semitones, none omitted — a material rather than a key
  • Whole Tone ScaleAll whole tones, no semitones, six notes — and therefore no tonic
  • Blues ScaleMinor pentatonic plus a lowered fifth — one note that turns smooth into gritty
  • Chinese Pentatonic ModesGong, shang, jiao, zhi, yu — five notes, and any one of them can be the tonic
  • Melodic Minor ScaleRaise the sixth and seventh going up, restore them coming down — a scale that differs by direction
  • Harmonic Minor ScaleNatural minor with a raised seventh — restoring the leading tone at the price of an augmented second
  • Locrian ModeA semitone above the tonic and a diminished fifth above it — the one mode that cannot stand still
  • Aeolian ModeStructurally identical to natural minor — the minor slot among the six modes
  • Mixolydian ModeMajor with a lowered seventh — major brightness, but without that compulsion to go home
  • Lydian ModeMajor with a raised fourth — the most suspended of the modes, with a tritone sitting above the tonic
  • Phrygian ModeNatural minor with a lowered second — a semitone sits right above the tonic, the most "exotic" colour of the modes
  • Dorian ModeNatural minor with a raised sixth — a minor colour, one shade brighter
  • Pentatonic ScaleA scale with no semitones at all — cut it anywhere and you never hit a minor second
  • Natural Minor ScaleWhole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole — a major scale with the 3rd, 6th and 7th lowered
  • Major ScaleWhole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half — seven notes and two semitone gaps, the default ground of tonal music
  • ScaleA scale is a selection of pitches out of twelve, put in order — the choices shape the style

T4

  • Chord ColourWhy one chord can sound bright here and murky there — register, voicing and doubling all play a part
  • Chord VoicingThe same notes, packed close or spread wide — and they can sound like different chords
  • Non-Chord ToneA note outside the current chord — which lets melody and harmony stop being one-to-one
  • Chord Inversion and Figured BassPut a different chord tone in the bass — the name holds, the sound and function change
  • Ninth, Eleventh and Thirteenth ChordsKeep stacking thirds — the fifth goes first, then the eleventh and thirteenth arrive
  • Sixth ChordA triad plus a sixth — one register away from a seventh chord, yet a different function
  • Added-Tone ChordAdd a note to an intact triad — without replacing anything and without stacking another third
  • Suspended ChordReplace the third with a second or a fourth — neither major nor minor, so it hangs
  • Half-Diminished SeventhA diminished triad plus a minor seventh — half diminished, and therefore milder than a full diminished seventh
  • Minor SeventhA minor triad plus a minor seventh — the gentlest and most widely used seventh chord
  • Major SeventhA major triad plus a major seventh — the seventh sits a semitone below the octave, which makes it the sharpest
  • Diminished SeventhFour equal minor thirds — only three forms exist, and each holds two tritones
  • Dominant SeventhA major triad plus a minor seventh — it hides a tritone, and that is the strongest push in tonal music
  • Seventh ChordOne more third on top of a triad — four notes, and the added seventh supplies the push
  • Augmented TriadTwo major thirds stacked — symmetrical, unanchored, and absent from every diatonic scale
  • Diminished TriadTwo minor thirds stacked — its fifth is squeezed flat, so it must resolve
  • Minor TriadA minor third under a major third — a major triad with its third dropped a semitone
  • Major TriadA major third under a minor third — the most stable chord in tonality
  • TriadTwo thirds stacked into three notes — four qualities that carry all of harmony
  • ChordThree or more notes sounding together — and stacked in thirds, which is the part that matters

T5

  • Treating Non-Chord TonesThe note is easy; making it sound intentional is not — four rules of treatment
  • Chromatic HarmonyWhen every semitone can be read as somebody's leading tone, the tonal centre starts to slip
  • Pedal PointOne note refuses to move while the harmony churns above it — that is how tension accumulates
  • Figured Bass and ContinuoOne bass line with figures telling you what to play above it — the Baroque turned accompaniment into readable code
  • Harmonic RhythmHow fast the chords change — it shapes momentum more than the chords themselves do
  • Leading-Tone SeventhA seventh chord built on the leading tone — the strongest push, with no modulation required
  • SequenceRepeat one figure at different pitch levels — the simplest way to keep music moving
  • TonicizationTreat another degree as the tonic for a moment — borrow a centre, then hand it back
  • Secondary DominantGive any degree its own temporary dominant — freshness without modulating
  • Circle of FifthsTwelve keys arranged by perfect fifths — signatures, close relations and functional chains all live on this one diagram
  • ModulationMoving the tonal centre — smooth between close keys, abrupt between distant ones, and it all depends on the transition
  • Parallel Fifths and OctavesThey are avoided not because they sound bad, but because they make two voices sound like one
  • Voice LeadingChords do not get swapped — each voice travels to the next one on its own
  • CadenceThe punctuation of harmony — authentic, plagal, half and deceptive, four ways to close
  • Degrees and Harmonic FunctionSeven degrees collapse into three functions — tonic, dominant, subdominant — the most economical way to organise tonal music
  • Tonal CentreOne pitch acts as home, and everything else is measured against it — that is tonality

T6

  • Cantus FirmusOne given melody as the frame, with everything else growing around it
  • Contrapuntal DevicesMirror, retrograde, augmentation, diminution — four transformations that each change one dimension
  • FugueOne subject enters in voice after voice, and then carries the whole piece
  • CanonOne melody followed faithfully to the end by another voice — imitation taken to its limit
  • Fifth SpeciesFlorid counterpoint — the first four mixed freely, fewest rules, hardest judgement
  • ImitationOne line says something, another says it back — the most economical device in polyphony
  • Fourth SpeciesSyncopated counterpoint — suspensions displace the accents, and the suspended chord comes from here
  • Third SpeciesFour notes against one — within a single beat, dissonances may now come in runs
  • Second SpeciesTwo notes against one — the weak beat may now be dissonant
  • First SpeciesThe strict one-to-one exercise — first get every single interval right
  • Note Against NoteOne note to one note — the most basic form of counterpoint, and the easiest to check
  • CounterpointSeveral melodies at once, each one a melody on its own, together making harmony

T7

  • Rhythm TrainingBuild an internal pulse first, then handle complexity — reversing the order is painful
  • Notating RhythmNote shapes show proportions, not seconds — the premise for reading any score
  • Metronome and BPMTurning speed from feeling into number — a calibration tool, not a performance standard
  • Free Metre and SanbanNo even pulse, yet rhythm remains — phrasing and tension simply live elsewhere
  • Rhythmic PatternA recurring combination of durations — the cheapest tool for character
  • Dance RhythmsThe dance shapes the rhythm, because the rhythm has to fit the feet
  • Polyrhythm and Cross RhythmTwo divisions running at once — the difference from syncopation is how many cycles there are
  • Tempo Terms and MarkingsFrom feeling words to beats per minute — the history of tempo terms is a history of performance practice
  • Simple, Compound and Irregular MetreOne question decides it — can each beat be split into two?
  • TupletThree notes where two belong — because the duration system divides by two, and three has no place
  • SyncopationMoving the accent off the strong beat — the tension comes from a delayed expectation
  • Metric AccentThe accent returns on a fixed cycle — that gives music a pulse, and makes it possible to disrupt
  • Metre and Time SignatureHow many beats per bar and which note gets the beat — the notation of the accent cycle
  • RhythmThe arrangement of durations — metre is the ruler, rhythm is how you actually walk

T8

  • Hearing MelodyHearing a melody means hearing relations, not absolute pitches — three layers from coarse to fine
  • Designing a ClimaxA climax is not the loudest moment but the release after accumulation
  • Breathing and PhrasingA melody breathes — which is what separates it from a run of notes
  • Thematic DevelopmentTake the subject apart, rework it, reassemble — development is not variation
  • Motive and SubjectA motive is material, a subject is a statement — they differ in completeness, not length
  • Phrase and PeriodThe melody's sentence and its pair of sentences — judged by the strength of cadences
  • The Making of a MelodyA scale is material; a melody is a selection from it arranged in time

T9

  • Free FormNo pre-existing pattern — but free does not mean formless
  • Concerto FormA dialogue between soloist and orchestra — the only form built on the individual against the collective
  • Large-Scale StructuresThe multi-movement layout of symphonies, sonatas and concertos — one long breath
  • Song FormVerse and chorus — the chorus returning repeatedly is a close relative of rondo form
  • SuiteA set of self-standing pieces arranged by some principle — the movements do not seek wholeness
  • Canon FormOrganising canonic writing into a whole piece — the structure is built from changes in the following rule
  • Fugue FormThe structure of a fugue: exposition, middle section, final section — it has its own sectional logic
  • One-Part FormOne idea standing alone — the baseline against which form itself is judged
  • Genre versus FormGenre asks what it is for; form asks how it is built — two questions, constantly confused
  • Variation FormA A₁ A₂ A₃ — one theme repeatedly re-dressed, its outline always recognisable
  • Compound Ternary FormTernary form scaled up — each section is itself a complete form
  • Rondo FormABACA — A keeps coming back while the episodes differ each time
  • Binary FormTwo sections, A and B — the basic shape of Baroque dances and the ancestor of sonata form
  • Ternary FormABA — out and back, the commonest form of all
  • Sonata FormNot merely three sections — its core is a tonality first split, then unified
  • Musical FormForm is how repetition, contrast and return are combined — not a mould but an organising principle

T10

  • Writing Time SignaturesWhere it goes, what the C sign means, and how to change metre mid-piece
  • Contemporary NotationWhen music stops centring on pitch and duration, the premises of traditional notation fail
  • OrnamentsThe signs are fixed; the execution changes with the era — literal reading is often wrong
  • Articulation MarksIt decides how notes connect — a different dimension from how loud they are
  • Ties and SlursNearly identical in shape, entirely different in meaning — the commonest misreading
  • Gongche NotationA system that writes pitch with Chinese characters — and its "ban-yan" idea still lives in modern terminology
  • Numbered Notation (Jianpu)Recording degrees with the digits 1–7 — easy to write and read, with its own trade-offs
  • Dynamic MarkingsDynamics are relative — there is no absolute volume, only relations
  • AccidentalsFive signs, one scope rule, three uses
  • RestsA rest is not blank space but part of the music — breath, expectation and clearing
  • Note ValuesThe practical reading and writing of note values — stems, flags, dots and beaming
  • Key SignatureA key signature sets defaults; it does not name a key — one signature serves several keys
  • ClefThe clef decides which line is which note — without it a staff means nothing
  • The StaffFive lines give positions, not pitches — a staff means nothing without a clef

T11

  • Hearing Intervals in ContextThe same interval means entirely different things in different positions in a key
  • Hearing RhythmFind the beat first, then the pattern — reversing the order makes a muddle
  • Hearing Key and ScaleThe test is where the centre is, not which notes are used
  • Hearing ChordsFunction matters more than quality — hearing "that is the dominant" beats hearing "that is major"
  • Sight-SingingSinging from the page — it forces you to pre-hear before you make a sound
  • Ear TrainingNot about naming notes, but about building a channel from hearing to knowing

T12

  • ImprovisationImprovisation is not unprepared — it is composing in real time from known material and rules
  • Historical PerformanceA score is incomplete information — it records what players of its time needed
  • Stage ExperienceExperience changes your ability to work with nerves, not the nerves themselves
  • Ensemble ListeningIn an ensemble, hearing others matters more than hearing yourself — yet beginners hear only themselves
  • Recording and Self-AssessmentA recording is the most objective teacher — while playing you hear your intention, not the sound
  • Posture and RelaxationRelaxation is not slackness but using effort only where it belongs
  • Sight-ReadingSight-reading trains one thing above all — not stopping
  • Hands SeparateIt reduces the amount to process at once — and combining is a new task in itself
  • Slow PracticeThe point of slow practice is not slowness but giving the correct action a chance to repeat
  • MemorisationMemory needs multiple backups — relying on one kind collapses the moment it is interrupted
  • Performance AnxietyIt cannot be removed, only managed — the goal is not calm but doing it right while nervous
  • How to PractiseYou practise the hard places, not the whole piece — repeating what is right, not what is wrong
