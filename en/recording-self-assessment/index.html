Recording and Self-Assessment

A recording is the most objective teacher — while playing you hear your intention, not the sound

1 min read27 characters条目PerformanceUpdated 2026-09-25

theoryperformancepractice

Related

Start with this entry's core, a counter-intuitive fact:

While playing, you hear your intention, not the sound you actually make.

The brain fills in the actual sound with "how I mean it to go" — so you feel the playing matched your imagination, while the recording says otherwise. This is not self-deception but an inevitable coupling of hearing and action.

Which makes a recording the most objective teacher: it takes the actual sound out of your hands and turns it into something you can hear repeatedly, without the pressure of real-time action.

A four-step procedure

StepDo this
1 recordrecord a complete run (not fragments — only the whole reveals structure and stamina)
2 let it restlisten later (hours, or the next day)
3 listen objectivelyuse the four layers below; do not think "I know that bit" as you go
4 write down specificsnotes that are actionable

Step 2 cannot be skipped: listening straight after playing, you hear through the memory of the actions just made, and attention lands on "where I struggled" rather than "how it sounds". After a gap, your ears become an audience's ears again.

Four layers to listen for

LayerWhat to hearTypical discovery
1 rhythmis it steady throughout (especially in hard passages and joins)?"I thought it was steady, but I was secretly speeding up in the hard places"
2 dynamicsdo the levels match the intention? (see dynamic markings)"the crescendo I wanted sounds like suddenly getting loud"
3 phrasingis the punctuation clear? (see breathing and phrasing)"it rushes forward and never lands at the phrase end"
4 timbreis it even, without superfluous impact? (see articulation)"the sound turns hard when it is loud"

Layer 1 delivers the biggest surprises: because the ear barely notices gradual acceleration (see metronome and BPM) — a recording is the only objective check.

Notes must be actionable

This is the entry's most practical rule:

Useless noteUseful note
"it was bad""the crescendo in bars 17–24 starts too late"
"rhythm was unsteady""I rushed half a beat at the join in bar 33"
"the sound was not ideal""bar 51: pressing too hard in the upper register, the sound turns hard"

The difference is actionability: a useful note points directly at the next practice session (the same requirement as "say exactly which beats" under how to practise).

A common mistake

Listening only for the wrong notes.

Only hunting errorsConsequence
you find where the technique failedbut miss how the whole thing sounds
—and a recording's greatest value is precisely the whole: phrasing, shape, direction

So listen once as an audience (what is the overall impression? do I want to keep listening?), and only on the second pass as an inspector. Reverse the order and you will hear nothing but the errors.

Diagram: the gap between intention and reality

Listen: intention versus actual

Your recording cannot be played here — but rhythm can run a small "intention versus actual" exercise: count four beats inwardly first, then press play. Most people find their inner four beats and the actual four are not the same length — the smallest example in the time dimension of the gap between intention and reality.

Common misconceptions

  • "I know how I play; no need to record." While playing you hear intention, which deviates systematically from reality.
  • "Listen back immediately." Let it rest first: straight afterwards, attention is still occupied by the memory of the actions.
  • "A recording is for finding mistakes." Its greatest value is the whole (phrasing, shape, direction), not only errors.
  • "Saying what was bad is enough." Be actionable (which bars, what problem), or next time you cannot target it.
