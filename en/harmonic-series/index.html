Harmonic Series

Integer multiples of a fundamental form an ideal harmonic series used to compare interval ratios and spectral partials

2 min readSound and PitchUpdated 2026-11-02

theoryacousticsbasics

The harmonic series consists of integer multiples of a fundamental. It explains the frequency ratios of octaves, pure fifths and just major triads and helps analyse timbre. It does not by itself rank the stability of every chord.

What is hidden inside one tone

An ideal harmonic tone contains components at f₀, 2f₀, 3f₀ and so on. Partial is the general term for a spectral component; a harmonic is a partial at an integer multiple of the fundamental. Bells, drums and real piano strings can have inharmonic partials. A pure sine wave has only one frequency, so not every sound contains a complete series.

The table gives ideal frequency positions, not equal prominence in every real sound. Partials differ in strength and audibility. Partial numbering includes the fundamental: partial 1 is the fundamental and partial 2 is the first overtone.

With the octave aligned, partial 3 is about 1.96 cents above equal-tempered G, partial 5 about 13.69 cents below E, and partial 7 about 31.17 cents below B♭. The discrepancy does not begin only at partial 7.

Four things it explains

One: why the octave is the most consonant. Partials 2, 4 and 8 all carry the same note name. Two tones an octave apart share the most partials, so they sound like "one note". See octave.

Two: the fifth’s ratio. Partials 3 and 2 form 3:2, and a pure fourth is 4:3. These are simple just ratios. “Perfect interval” is a music-theoretical category, not a guarantee that every performance uses those exact frequencies.

Three: the just major triad. Partials 4, 5 and 6 form 4:5:6, approximately notated C–E–G for a C root. The major third is 5:4 and the minor third 6:5. Equal-tempered C–E–G approximates rather than exactly reproduces these frequencies; tonal context and bass still determine harmonic stability.

Four: where timbre comes from. Partials differ in strength. A strong fundamental with weak upper partials sounds round; prominent upper partials sound bright or piercing. How the partials behave in the first instant decides the instrument's attack. That is the physical floor under timbre.

Listen: picking out the partials

The demonstration plays C3, C4, G4, C5, E5, G5, B♭5 and C6 as equal-tempered approximations to the first eight partials. Octaves align exactly; partials 3, 5, 6 and 7 already differ, with partial 7 showing a particularly noticeable discrepancy.

Common misconceptions

  • Overtones are not inherently inaudible: audible partials often fuse into one timbre and can be highlighted by technique or filtering.
  • Timbre depends on attack, decay, noise and changes over time as well as the static strengths of partials.
  • Piano stretch tuning principally accounts for string-stiffness inharmonicity, not for the difference between partial 7 and B♭.
  • An ideal harmonic series is a model; real sounds can have weak or missing harmonics and non-integer partials.
