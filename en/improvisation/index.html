Improvisation

Improvisation is not unprepared — it is composing in real time from known material and rules

3 min readPerformanceUpdated 2026-09-25

theoryperformancepractice

Related

Figured BassOrnamentsHistorical Performance PracticeHearing ChordsCadence

The last misconception is also the most interesting:

Improvisation is not "being unprepared" but "composing in real time from known material and rules".

It requires more preparation than composing does — because there is no chance to revise: a composer can change things repeatedly, an improviser must get it right once.

Historically, improvisation was the norm, not the exception

OccasionWhat was improvised
continuosupplying the upper parts live from the figures (see figured bass)
ornamentsadded at will by convention (see ornaments)
cadenzasthe soloist dazzling on the spot (see concerto form)
preludes / fantasiasoften improvisatory in character (as in the Bach fantasia among the instances)

The score was "skeleton plus cue", and the player completed it live — exactly the conclusion reached under historical performance.

"Improvisation has been lost" needs qualifying: it declined in classical music after the nineteenth century (as notation became ever more explicit), but in jazz and many folk traditions it has always been central.

Improvisation and composing are not two things

The most important recognition here:

ComposingImprovising
Materialexisting material and stylistic conventionthe same
Constraintsharmony, form, stylethe same
Outputwritten down, revisableplayed directly, not revisable

Both are "making choices under constraints" — the difference is only the chance to revise and the time available. So being able to improvise does not mean working without rules; on the contrary, it demands a more fluent command of them, since there is no time to think.

Three levels (also a practice path)

LevelDo thisDepends on
1 ornamentadd ornaments to a known melodyperformance convention (see ornaments)
2 textureorganise the voices live over a harmonic framejudging harmonic function (see chord hearing)
3 melodyinvent melody over a progressiontonal hearing and shaping material (see interval hearing)

Level 1 is the easiest entry: ornamenting a familiar melody is the first form of improvisation. Levels 2 and 3 approach what is usually meant by improvising, and both presuppose judging harmonic direction in real time.

Why it is worth learning (even if you never improvise)

GainExplanation
understanding the composer's situationknowing "which places were left to the player then"
better sight-readingimprovisation trains prediction (see sight-reading)
more active listeningthe improviser's ear is finding a way, not following one
music as more than executionfrom "turning the page into sound" to "choosing within the rules"

The fourth is fundamental: it changes your relationship to music — from executor to chooser. And knowing that you are making a choice is exactly the landing point of historical performance.

Diagram: the shared structure of composing and improvising

Listen: many routes through one frame

Improvisation is making different choices over the same frame. Use progression on a standard progression — one set of chords admits countless melodies and textures, which is exactly where improvisation has room.

Common misconceptions

  • "Improvisation means playing anything." The opposite: choosing in real time under constraints, with no chance to revise, which demands more fluency.
  • "Improvisation has been lost." It declined in classical music, but has always been central in jazz and folk traditions.
  • "Improvisation and composing are separate." The material and constraints are identical; only revisability differs.
  • "No need to learn it if I will not improvise." It trains prediction and active listening, directly improving sight-reading and understanding of works.
