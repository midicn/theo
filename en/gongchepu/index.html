Gongche Notation

A system that writes pitch with Chinese characters — and its "ban-yan" idea still lives in modern terminology

Related

Gongche notation is the principal notational system of traditional Chinese music, and it writes pitch with Chinese characters.

Pitches written as characters

The usual characters are 上 · 尺 · 工 · 凡 · 六 · 五 · 乙 (with lower characters such as 合 · 四 · 一).

One distinction matters:

The same character sounds at different pitch under different "men" (modal keys).

So a gongche character is closer to a position in a system of note names, its actual pitch fixed by the modal key (which is closely tied to the instrument, genre and region). That is functionally comparable to numbered notation's 1 = C, but the mechanism differs.

Hold on to this and gongche notation will not be confused with numbered notation.

Ban and yan: its metre marking

This is its most living part:

SignMeaningEquivalent to
ban (板)strong beatthe strong position in modern terms
yan (眼)weak beatthe weak position

The terminology it produced is still in everyday use:

TermMeaning
yi ban yi yantwo beats per bar (one strong, one weak)
yi ban san yanfour beats per bar (one strong, three weak)
you ban you yan"with ban and yan" — well measured and proper; the phrase has entered ordinary Chinese
sanbanno fixed ban or yan (see free metre and sanban)
liushuibana fast one-beat metre

That table deserves attention: it shows gongche notation is not a museum piece — its core concepts (ban = strong, yan = weak) have entered modern Chinese, and phrases like "you ban you yan" describe exactly this system.

Its manner of writing and its trade-offs

FeatureExplanation
vertical, right to leftfollowing the direction of classical Chinese writing (opposite to staff and numbered notation)
modal key stated at the startit fixes the sounding pitch of the characters
durations recorded looselydurations are often filled in from the ban-yan positions and oral transmission
what it records wellsingle-line vocal and instrumental melody (opera arias, instrumental pieces)

The third row is the key one: gongche notation could not record exact durations — rather, the tradition it serves relies on oral transmission. The notation is "frame and cue", not "complete instruction".

That is the exact opposite of the historical direction of Western notation, which has tended to write every detail out (see the history in the staff and the metronome), while gongche notation keeps the combination of notation plus oral transmission.

So neither can be called "more advanced" — the two choices serve different musical practices and modes of transmission.

Listen: a single melodic line

What gongche notation records is a single line. Use scale on a pentatonic span — pentatonicism is the common basis of traditional Chinese melody (see the Chinese pentatonic scale).

Common misconceptions

  • "Gongche notation is the ancestor of numbered notation." They record different things: gongche uses characters for pitch, fixed by the modal key; numbered notation uses digits for degrees.
  • "Gongche notation is crude." The tradition it serves relies on oral transmission: the notation is frame and cue. Less precision does not mean backwardness.
  • "Ban and yan are obsolete." Quite the opposite: the core concept has entered modern Chinese (you ban you yan, yi ban yi yan, sanban).
  • "Gongche notation can record complex multi-part music." Its strength is single-line vocal and instrumental melody; complex textures belong to the staff.
  • "Gongche is the only character-based Chinese notation." They record different things: one pitch, one rhythm. Melody and sung parts use gongche; percussion has its own system — luogu jing — which uses onomatopoeic characters (仓 · 才 · 台 · 大 · 嘟 · 乙) to record rhythm and stroke; see the xiaoluo. Both use characters, and both are learned by chanting them aloud.
