Ear training is often mistaken for "training absolute pitch". Its real goal is something else:

To build a channel from hearing to knowing. Hear a note and you can say where it sits in the key; hear a chord and you know where it is heading.

Absolute pitch (hearing "that is A4") is a bonus, not a requirement — it is in fact hard to train effectively; relative pitch (hearing relations) can be acquired through practice. So the correct goal was never "naming notes".

Four layers

LayerWhat you hearExample
1 relationsdistance and direction between notes"that is a step", "that leapt a sixth"
2 structurephrases, sections, where the closes fall"that is the end of a phrase" (see [[concept:phrase
3 functionwhat the harmony is doing"that stopped on the dominant and continues" (see [[concept:harmonic-function
4 predictionseeing the page and knowing roughly how it soundsthe precondition for sight-reading and sight-singing

Layer 4 is ear training's graduation standard: not knowing after hearing, but predicting from seeing. Which is also the core purpose of sight-singing (see sight-singing).

Layer 2 is the most practical: being able to hear "that sentence has finished" directly determines whether you can count your entries in an ensemble (see ensemble listening).

Four effective methods

MethodPracticeWhy it works
with your instrumentplay and listen, listen and findbinds the sound to an action or position
singsing what you hearsinging is the only way to verify that you really heard it
dictationwrite down what you hearif you cannot write it, you have not heard it
analyse real worksuse real repertoire, not exercisesreal music contains complexity and exceptions

The second row is the most undervalued: many people "feel they heard it" but cannot sing it accurately — which means they did not hear it. Singing is ear training's lie detector.

The fourth row matters too: exercise material (isolated intervals, say) is decontextualised, whereas in real music an interval's meaning depends on its context (see hearing intervals).

Listen: start from relations

Use interval to hear one interval — the first step in ear training is asking "which number, which quality", not "which note name". Hear it, name the relation inwardly, then play the second note to check.

Common misconceptions

  • "Ear training means absolute pitch." The opposite: you train relations. Absolute pitch is a bonus and hard

    • to train.

  • "Ear training is a gift." Relative pitch can be acquired by practice; method matters more than talent.
  • "Ear training needs dedicated sessions." All four methods fit into daily life: listen while you practise,

    • analyse while you listen, sing at any time.

  • "Being able to hear it is enough." Singing it accurately and writing it down are the tests. "It felt

    • clear" is often an illusion. :::
