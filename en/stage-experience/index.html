Start with an expectation that must be corrected:

Stage experience changes not whether you are nervous but your ability to work with nerves.

Professional performers are just as nervous — they simply know what to do about it. So "perform enough and you will stop being nervous" is the wrong expectation; the right one is "perform enough and you will be better at doing it right while nervous" (see performance anxiety).

What the stage changes

The difference from the practice room is not your technique but the conditions:

ConditionDifferenceEffect
acousticsdifferent reverberation, you hear yourself differentlydynamics and tempo need adjusting
instrumentoften not the one you knowdifferent touch and feedback
distancesound returns with a delayeasy to push forward
being watchedattention turns inward (see below)—
no stoppingyou cannot start againan error must be carried on

The first row is the most overlooked: a reverberant hall makes you hear a delay, so you unconsciously play faster and faster — a classic stage mishap, unrelated to your technique.

Three practical preparations

PreparationMethod
1 reliable entry pointsa clear starting place for each section, so a blank can be recovered instantly
2 multiple memory backupsdo not rely on muscle memory (see [[concept:memorization\
3 practising performingpractise with someone listening (one person, or a recording)

The first has the highest priority: performances usually fail not because a note went wrong but because the player cannot rejoin afterwards (they can only start over — which the stage does not allow).

The third is the only way to train "the stage" directly — nothing else simulates being watched.

Handling mishaps: three common cases

MishapCorrect response
a wrong notecarry on — a wrong note affects one instant, stopping affects a whole passage
a blank (memory lapse)jump at once to the nearest entry point; do not try to "find where you were"
general disarrayreturn to the beat: find the pulse first, then your place

The second is the heart of stage experience: the worst choice after a blank is to stand still and reconstruct — reconstruction takes time and musical time keeps moving. Jumping to the nearest entry point is the only workable plan, which is exactly why entry points must be prepared in advance.

The third works as in ensemble playing: the beat is the last lifeline (see ensemble listening) — as long as the pulse survives, you can find your place again.

Two suggestions about "performing more"

SuggestionReason
perform often in informal settingsaccumulate "being watched" experience at low risk
write notes immediately afterwardswhile the memory is fresh, record where you panicked and why (see [[concept:recording-self-assessment\

The first is the most effective: real performances are too few and too costly, whereas being watched can be practised on any occasion with a listener — play for a classmate, for family, or treat recording as "someone listening".

Diagram: the stage changes conditions, not technique

Listen: the mechanism of a tempo slide

Use rhythm at two tempos — and notice that having settled into one, hearing a faster one pulls you along. The stage's "reverberation delay makes you speed up" runs on the same mechanism: your reference has changed.

Common misconceptions

  • "Perform enough and you stop being nervous." Experience changes the ability to work with nerves, not their

    • intensity. Professionals are just as nervous.

  • "A mistake is the end." Failing to rejoin is. Which is why entry points come first.
  • "A bad performance means insufficient practice." Often the conditions differ (acoustics, instrument,

    • attention) — practise under similar conditions.

  • "Only a real concert counts as performing." Any occasion with a listener builds "being watched" experience

    • at lower risk. :::
